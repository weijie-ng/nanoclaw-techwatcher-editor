## What it is

Vox's original house style, now the default for "what is X" pieces everywhere.
The headline is framed as a question, and the body is a sequence of
sub-questions with direct answers under each.

There is no narrative arc at all. It is a retrieval structure: readers arrive
with one question, land on the sub-heading that matches it, read that answer,
and leave. Sections must therefore be independently readable and safely
skippable.

## Use it when / do not use it when

Use it when the reader arrives already asking something: a new regime, a new
treaty, an unfamiliar mechanism, a term suddenly in the news. Use it when the
audience is mixed, so that experts can skip to section six while newcomers read
from the top. Use it when the piece will be found by search rather than read
in a feed.

Do not use it when there is a genuine narrative or a development to report,
where the question format flattens the news out of the story. Do not use it
when you have no real questions and are inventing sub-heads to fill a shape:
that produces the worst version of this format. Do not use it for a piece
making an argument, which wants `narrated-digest` or `anecdotal-lede`.

## The skeleton

| Block | Job | Budget | Required |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question headline | The exact question a reader would type | 1 line | Yes |
| Standfirst | The one-sentence answer, before any sub-heads | 1 to 2 sentences | Yes |
| **What is it?** | Definition, plainly, with no assumed vocabulary | 2 to 3 sentences | Yes |
| **What just happened?** | The news peg that brought the reader here | 2 to 4 sentences | Yes |
| **How does it work?** | The mechanism, concretely | 1 to 2 short paragraphs or a numbered list | Yes |
| **Who does it affect?** | Named groups, with the effect on each | Bullets or 1 paragraph | Yes |
| **Why is it controversial?** | Both readings, stated fairly | 1 to 2 paragraphs | Yes if disputed |
| **What happens next?** | Dated milestones | 2 to 3 sentences | Yes if any exist |
| **Where can I read more?** | Primary sources, not coverage | 2 to 4 links | Optional |

Sub-heads are written as real questions, in the reader's words, not as topic
labels. "How does it work?" not "Mechanism". Order them by how likely a reader
is to ask them, most likely first.

## Worked example

```
# What is the EU's new dual-use export regime, and who does it hit?

The EU has extended export licensing to a class of civilian electronics and
shifted the compliance burden onto intermediaries rather than manufacturers.

## What is a dual-use good?
An item with both civilian and military applications...

## What just happened?
On 14 March the Council adopted its nineteenth package...

## How does the licensing work?
1. The exporter classifies the item against the control list.
2. If it is listed, a licence is sought from the national authority.
3. The authority checks the stated end user against...

## Who does it affect?
- **Brokers and freight forwarders.** Now liable for end-user checks...
- **Candidate-country firms.** Listed for the first time...

## Why is it controversial?
Supporters argue... Opponents, including two member states that blocked the
package for four weeks, argue...

## What happens next?
Member states report enforcement measures within 30 days...
```

## Rhythm and length

Total 700 to 1,500 words, but the number that matters is per section: 60 to
150 words each. Answer in the first sentence of a section, then support it.
Never make the reader read a section to reach its answer.

No connective prose between sections. "Now that we have covered what it is,
let us turn to" is exactly what this format exists to avoid.

## Failure modes

- **Sub-heads that are labels, not questions.** "Background", "Analysis",
  "Context" are labels. The format dies without real questions.
- **Invented questions.** If nobody would ask it, it is padding. Six real
  questions beat eleven with five made up.
- **Narrative smuggled in.** Sections that only make sense read in order have
  broken the retrieval contract.
- **Burying the answer.** A section that spends three sentences on preamble
  fails the reader who jumped straight to it.
- **Assumed vocabulary in the first section.** If "dual-use" appears in the
  definition of dual-use, start again.

## Citations

Both layers, as set out in [citations.md](citations.md): inline attribution in
the prose plus a numbered marker to a source list. Source list placement for
this format: The `Where can I read more?` section becomes the source list.

Prefer primary sources throughout: this format is read by people who will go
and look. The definition section rarely needs markers; every section after it
does.

## Self-check

- [ ] Headline is a question a reader would actually type
- [ ] The standfirst answers it in one sentence
- [ ] Every sub-head is phrased as a question
- [ ] Every section answers in its first sentence
- [ ] Any section can be read alone, out of order
- [ ] No connective prose between sections
- [ ] No jargon used before it is defined
- [ ] The final section is the numbered source list, and primary sources are cited over coverage
