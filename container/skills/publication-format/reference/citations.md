## The rule

Nothing factual appears in a finished piece without a reader being able to get
back to where it came from. A piece that cannot be checked is not publishable,
whatever its shape.

This is not a stylistic preference. An agent-written piece will be read by
someone who was not present for the reporting and has no way to tell a sourced
number from an invented one. The citation is what makes the difference visible.

## The two layers

Every piece carries both. They do different jobs and neither replaces the other.

**Layer one, inline attribution.** The claim names its source in the sentence,
in the format's own voice. This is what makes the piece readable as journalism
and it is what a reader who never clicks anything still gets.

**Layer two, a numbered marker and a source list.** A bracketed marker after
the claim, pointing to a numbered entry at the end of the piece. This is what
makes the claim checkable.

```
28 of the 42 listed entities are intermediaries rather than manufacturers,
according to Council figures [1].
```

The prose says who; the marker says exactly where. Dropping layer one leaves a
piece of naked numbers with footnotes. Dropping layer two leaves a reader who
knows the Council said something but cannot find it.

## What needs a citation

Every one of these:

- Any number, date, quantity, proportion or currency figure.
- Any event asserted to have happened, with its date.
- Any statement of what a person, company, government or document said.
- Any direct quote.
- Any characterisation of a document's contents.
- Anything a reasonable reader might dispute.

These do not:

- Common knowledge in the piece's own field, at the level of "the EU has 27
  member states".
- Definitions the piece supplies itself.
- The writer's own analysis, inference or argument, provided it is visibly the
  writer's. An unmarked inference presented in the same register as a sourced
  fact is the failure this rule exists to prevent. Signal it in the prose: "on
  this reading", "what this suggests", "I think".

## Marker placement

- After the claim it supports, before the full stop: `... reached EUR 1.2bn
  last year [1].`
- At clause level when one sentence carries two sourced claims: `Transits fell
  14 per cent [2] while insurance costs rose [3].`
- One source per marker. Two sources for the same claim take two markers,
  `[1][4]`, not one entry listing both.
- **One link per claim.** The marker is the link. A claim that already carries
  a marker never also carries a separate inline link to the same place, which
  is two things to click and one destination.
- The same source reused later keeps its original number. Numbers are assigned
  in order of first appearance and never reused for a different source.
- Never place a marker on a heading, a label, or the writer's own inference.

## Source list entries

One line each, numbered, in first-appearance order:

```
[1] Council of the European Union, Decision (EU) 2025/412, Official Journal
    L 84/12, 14 March 2025, Annex II. <link>
[2] Regional Port Authority, Monthly Transit Statistics, February 2025,
    table 3. <link>
[3] Interview with Kaja Virtanen, Council sanctions coordinator, Brussels,
    14 March 2025.
```

Each entry carries, in this order: who published it, what it is, when, the
locator that gets the reader to the specific claim (annex, table, page,
timestamp), and a link if one exists.

The locator is the part most often dropped and the part that does the work.
"Council Decision" sends a reader to sixty pages; "Annex II" sends them to the
listing. Cite the page, not the document.

**Hyperlink the source itself.** Where the entry has a URL, the entry text is
the link. Do not append a separate "Link", "here" or "source" label: that is a
second thing to click for the same destination, and it tells the reader
nothing the entry text has not already told them.

```
[1] Council of the European Union, Decision (EU) 2025/412, ...   <- the whole
                                                                    entry links
[3] Author's count across Council Decisions 2024/1988, ...       <- no link
```

Entries with no link are not an oversight, and the difference is information:
a linked entry is something the reader can open, an unlinked one is an
interview, a document obtained privately, or the writer's own working. Leaving
those unlinked is how the reader tells the two apart at a glance.

Not everything has a URL. An interview, a document not published online, a
figure supplied on request are all citable without a link, provided the entry
says what it was and when.

## Where the source list goes, by format

| Format | Placement |
|---|---|
| `smart-brevity` | After the `Go deeper` line, under a `Sources` heading |
| `inverted-pyramid` | End of the piece, after the background tail |
| `anecdotal-lede` | End of the piece, after the return |
| `martini-glass` | End of the piece, after the close |
| `explainer-faq` | The `Where can I read more?` section becomes the source list |
| `delayed-reveal` | End of the piece. The provenance paragraph stays in the body: it explains how the documents were obtained and verified, which a source list cannot do |
| `top-story-digest` | End of the piece. Digest items carry a marker like everything else, not a separate inline link |
| `curated-list` | No separate list. Each item's source line is its citation, and markers are not used, since every item is already one source |
| `thematic-clusters` | End of the piece, after the watch list |
| `narrated-digest` | End of the piece, under `Sources` |

`curated-list` is the only exception, and it is exempt because the format is
already nothing but citations. Every other format carries both layers.

## Quotes, documents and estimates

**Quotes** need attribution in the prose without exception, naming the speaker
and their standing on first use, plus a marker pointing to where the quote is
recorded: a transcript, a briefing, a filing, an interview.

**Documents** are cited by their identifier, not by the coverage of them. If a
newspaper reported what a decision says and the decision is public, cite the
decision. Cite the reporting only when the reporting is itself the source, as
with an interview nobody else conducted.

**Estimates, projections and disputed figures** are labelled as such in the
prose, and the citation points to whoever produced them: "Council estimates",
"the company's own projection". A projection cited as a fact is a
misattribution even when the number is copied correctly.

**A source that is itself reporting a source** gets both where it matters:
name the original in the prose, cite what you actually read.

## Claims you cannot source

Sometimes the material will not support a claim the draft wants to make. In
descending order of preference:

1. Cut the claim. Usually the piece does not need it.
2. Reduce it to what the source does support, and cite that.
3. State the gap in the piece: "the number of affected firms has not been
   published", "the ministry did not respond to questions about the timeline".
   A stated gap is information; a silent one is a hole.

What is never acceptable: keeping the claim and hoping, inventing a plausible
source, citing a real source that does not actually say it, or citing a source
you have not read. A fabricated or mismatched citation is worse than no
citation, because it converts an unverified claim into an apparently verified
one.

If a claim matters and cannot be sourced, that is a reporting problem, not a
formatting problem. Say so rather than papering over it.

## Failure modes

- **Markers without inline attribution.** Numbered brackets scattered through
  prose that never names anyone. Checkable but unreadable.
- **Inline attribution without markers.** "Officials said" with no way to find
  which officials, where.
- **A citation that does not support the sentence it sits on.** The commonest
  serious error, and the hardest for a reader to catch. Check each marker
  against its claim, not against the paragraph.
- **Document-level locators.** Citing a sixty-page decision for a single
  figure buried in an annex.
- **Renumbered sources after an edit.** Moving a paragraph and leaving the
  markers behind breaks every number after it. Renumber last, and check.
- **Analysis wearing a citation.** A marker on the writer's own inference
  borrows authority the source never gave it.
- **Uncited quotes.** A quote with a speaker but no record of where it was said.
- **A marker and a bare link side by side.** Two links, one destination. Keep
  the marker.

## Self-check

Run this against the draft before handing over, in addition to the format's own
checks.

- [ ] Every number, date, quantity and quoted statement carries a marker
- [ ] Every marker's source actually supports the specific claim it sits on
- [ ] Every claim also names its source in the prose, in the format's voice
- [ ] Every source entry has a locator, not just a document title
- [ ] Numbers run in first-appearance order with no gaps and no reuse
- [ ] Every source cited was actually read, and none was invented
- [ ] The writer's own inferences carry no markers and are visibly inferences
- [ ] Claims that could not be sourced were cut, reduced, or stated as gaps
- [ ] No claim carries both a marker and a separate link to the same source
- [ ] Source entries link on the entry text, with no "Link" label appended
