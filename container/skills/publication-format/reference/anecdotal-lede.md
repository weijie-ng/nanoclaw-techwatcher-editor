## What it is

The Wall Street Journal formula. The piece opens on one specific person or
scene, held for two or three paragraphs, then pulls back to the thesis in the
**nut graf**, usually paragraph two or three. The rest of the piece alternates
between the wider argument and returns to specific cases, and closes back on a
person, often the one it opened with.

The Information, Wired features and Verge features all run on this. It is the
default structure for a trend piece.

## Use it when / do not use it when

Use it for features and trend pieces: something is changing, you can show it
happening to someone, and the change is the story rather than any single event.
Use it when the reader has no reason to care yet and needs a person to care
about first.

Do not use it for breaking news. A reader who wants to know what happened will
not wait three paragraphs, and an editor cutting for space will cut your
opening scene and leave the piece headless: that is `inverted-pyramid`. Do not
use it when you do not actually have a specific, reported, named case. A
composite or an invented "imagine a customs broker" opening is not this format,
it is a bad essay.

## The skeleton

| Block | Job | Budget | Required |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anecdotal lede | One named person or one specific scene, concrete detail, present tense pressure | 2 to 3 paragraphs | Yes |
| Turn | The sentence that widens from this person to the pattern | 1 sentence | Yes |
| **Nut graf** | The thesis: what is changing, how widely, why it matters now | 1 to 2 paragraphs, usually para 2 to 3 | Yes |
| Evidence | Numbers, named sources, scope of the pattern | 2 to 4 paragraphs | Yes |
| Second case | A different person or place showing the same thing | 1 to 2 paragraphs | Optional but usual |
| Counter-case | Who it is not happening to, or who disputes the pattern | 1 to 2 paragraphs | Yes |
| Causes | Why now: the policy, the ruling, the incentive that changed | 2 to 3 paragraphs | Yes |
| Return | Back to the opening person, moved forward in time | 1 to 2 paragraphs | Yes |

The nut graf is the load-bearing block. Everything before it earns attention;
everything after it spends attention. If a reader stops after the nut graf they
should still know what the piece is about.

## Worked example

```
Marta Ilves runs a customs brokerage out of two rooms above a tyre shop in
Narva, forty metres from the Russian border. For nineteen years her work was
paperwork: forms, tariff codes, the occasional argument about a pallet of
machine parts.

Last spring she started refusing clients. By September she had turned away
eleven of them, four with contracts she had held for a decade. "You look at the
end user and you cannot see the end," she said. "So you say no, and you lose
the money."

Brokers like Ilves have become the enforcement layer of European sanctions
policy, a role no legislation assigned them and no state pays for. As listed
entities multiply and penalties shift onto intermediaries, the practical
decision about whether a shipment moves is increasingly taken by a small firm
in a border town with no legal department.

Filings reviewed for this article show 1,400 brokerages operating on the EU's
eastern frontier, 62 per cent of them with fewer than five employees...
```

## Rhythm and length

Total 900 to 2,000 words. Sentences in the lede are concrete and specific: a
place, a number, an object, a thing said. Sentences in the nut graf are the
opposite, abstract and declarative, and the shift between the two registers is
what makes the turn work.

Do not linger. Two or three paragraphs of scene is the budget. Four is an essay
about a person that never became a piece about a pattern.

## Failure modes

- **The nut graf never lands.** The commonest failure. The piece stays with the
  person and the reader never learns what it is arguing. Write the nut graf
  first, then the scene that earns it.
- **The scene is decorative.** If the opening person does not embody the thesis,
  they are throat-clearing. Cut and find one who does.
- **Nut graf too late.** Past paragraph five, most readers have gone.
- **A composite or hypothetical opening.** "Picture a shipping agent" is not a
  lede. If the reporting does not supply a real case, use `explainer-faq`.
- **No return.** Ending on the abstract argument wastes the person you
  introduced and leaves the piece unfinished.

## Citations

Both layers, as set out in [citations.md](citations.md): inline attribution in
the prose plus a numbered marker to a source list. Source list placement for
this format: End of the piece, after the return.

The opening scene needs citing like anything else: how the reporting
establishes that Ilves turned away eleven clients matters more here than
elsewhere, because the scene is doing the persuading. The nut graf's scope
claim is usually the piece's most contestable sentence and always needs a
marker.

## Self-check

- [ ] The opening person or scene is real, named or specifically sourced
- [ ] The nut graf appears by paragraph four
- [ ] The nut graf states what is changing, how widely, and why now
- [ ] The opening case actually demonstrates the nut graf's claim
- [ ] There is a counter-case, not just supporting cases
- [ ] The piece returns to the opening person at the end
- [ ] A reader stopping at the nut graf knows what the piece is about
- [ ] The opening scene and the nut graf's scope claim both carry markers
