## What it is

A common investigative structure. It buries the lede deliberately: credibility,
context and the state of the record are established first, and the damning fact
is held back for a reveal several paragraphs in.

The reason is not suspense. It is that a serious allegation stated cold reads
as an accusation, and the same allegation stated after the reader knows the
documents exist, who verified them and what the subject was asked reads as a
finding. The delay buys the fact its weight.

Rare in daily news, standard in enterprise reporting: leaked documents,
whistleblower accounts, procurement and sanctions-evasion investigations.

## Use it when / do not use it when

Use it when the finding is contestable, the sourcing needs explaining before it
will be believed, and the subject has been given a right of reply. Use it when
the reader's likely first reaction is "how would anyone know that".

Do not use it when the fact speaks for itself and is already public: holding
back what the reader already knows is a trick, not a structure, and readers
resent it. Do not use it for a story where the sequence rather than the finding
carries the interest, which is `martini-glass`. Never use it to manufacture
drama around a thin finding; if the reveal is weak, the delay makes it look
weaker.

## The skeleton

| Block | Job | Budget | Required |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anchoring lede | The subject, the setting, and a fact that is true but not yet the finding | 1 to 2 paragraphs | Yes |
| Stakes | Why this subject matters: scale, money, public role | 1 to 2 paragraphs | Yes |
| The record | What is officially claimed, in the subject's own words or filings | 2 to 3 paragraphs | Yes |
| Provenance | What documents exist, how they were obtained, how they were verified | 1 to 2 paragraphs | Yes |
| **The reveal** | The finding, stated flatly, no adjectives | 1 short paragraph | Yes |
| Corroboration | The second and third source that make it stand up | 2 to 4 paragraphs | Yes |
| Response | What the subject said when asked, quoted at length, or that they declined | 1 to 2 paragraphs | Yes |
| Consequence | What follows: legal exposure, regulatory duty, who must now act | 2 to 3 paragraphs | Yes |

The reveal is stated in the plainest possible language. Every adjective in that
paragraph weakens it. Let the preceding sourcing do the arguing.

## Worked example

```
ANCHORING LEDE
Baltic Meridian Shipping operates eleven vessels from a single office in
Limassol. Until 2022 it moved fertiliser.

STAKES
It now holds charter contracts worth EUR 210m a year, and three of its vessels
call at EU ports monthly.

THE RECORD
In its filings and in a written statement in January, the company said it had
"no counterparties in listed jurisdictions" and that all end users were
verified against the consolidated list.

PROVENANCE
Documents reviewed for this article, comprising 2,300 pages of charter records
and bunkering invoices from 2022 to 2024, were provided by a person with access
to the company's systems and verified against port-call data and two
insurers' records.

THE REVEAL
The records show 41 voyages in which cargo listed as fertiliser was discharged
at a terminal operated by a sanctioned entity.

CORROBORATION
Port-call data independently places the vessels...

RESPONSE
Asked about the 41 voyages, the company said... A director declined to answer
specific questions but said...
```

## Rhythm and length

Total 1,500 to 3,500 words. Reveal at 30 to 45 per cent of the way through:
earlier feels rushed given the setup, later and readers leave. Sentences before
the reveal are careful and qualified; the reveal itself is the shortest
sentence in the piece.

Attribute constantly. In this format the sourcing is the argument.

## Failure modes

- **A weak reveal.** If the held-back fact turns out to be ordinary, the whole
  structure reads as manipulation. Test it: state the reveal cold to someone.
  If they shrug, restructure as `inverted-pyramid`.
- **Delaying past the reader's patience.** Half the piece as throat-clearing
  loses everyone before the payoff.
- **Adjectives on the reveal.** "Damning", "shocking", "brazen" all tell the
  reader what to feel and invite them to disagree.
- **Response buried at the bottom.** The subject's answer belongs immediately
  after the corroboration, not as a final line of boilerplate.
- **Provenance vagueness.** "Documents seen by this publication" without saying
  what kind, how many, or how verified, will not carry a contested finding.

## Citations

Both layers, as set out in [citations.md](citations.md): inline attribution in
the prose plus a numbered marker to a source list. Source list placement for
this format: End of the piece.

The provenance paragraph stays in the body and is not replaced by the source
list: it says how the documents were obtained and verified, which no entry in
a list can convey. The reveal itself carries the heaviest citation in the
piece, and the subject's prior claim is cited from their own filing or
statement, not from someone's report of it.

## Self-check

- [ ] The reveal would still be news if stated in the first line
- [ ] The reveal paragraph carries no adjectives and no adverbs
- [ ] Provenance says what the documents are, how obtained, how verified
- [ ] The subject's own prior claim is quoted before the reveal contradicts it
- [ ] The subject was asked, and their response or refusal appears in full
- [ ] At least two independent sources corroborate the finding
- [ ] The reveal lands between 30 and 45 per cent of the way through
- [ ] The reveal and the subject's prior claim are both cited to primary documents, and provenance remains in the body
