Publication format

Ten skeletons at a glance

Every format below is a fixed order of blocks: read top to bottom, that is the order the reader meets them. Each block is drawn as a labelled box whose width is its span and whose height is its length, so a card reads as the format's shape, block by block. Six carry one subject; four carry several. Optional blocks are cut, never reordered. Point at any format and ask me to explain it in depth, or to draft your piece in one.

Article formats

One subject, told one way. A newsletter's lead item may itself be written to one of these; an article never contains a newsletter.

Smart Brevity

Reader needs the takeaway fast and may act on it.

Axiom headline6 words or fewer
Strong lede1 short sentence
Why it matters1 to 2 sentences
Driving the news2 to 4 bullets
By the numbers3 to 5 figures
The big picture2 to 3 sentences
What they're saying1 to 2 quotes
The other side1 to 2 sentences
What's next1 to 2 sentences if present
Go deeper1 line

Pros

  • Reader takes the headline and the first label and leaves with the point intact; blocks are readable out of order.
  • Significance before detail: 'Why it matters' lands the consequence before the narrative.
  • Modular, so optional blocks are cut cleanly and the shape scales down to what you actually have.

Cons

  • 'Why it matters' easily decays into a restatement of the lede, the commonest failure of the format.
  • With a single fact it becomes four blocks restating the same sentence.
  • The labels fight a story: if the interest is how it unfolded, the format flattens it out.

Use cases

  • A policy note or announcement a busy person reads on a phone between meetings.
  • A briefing where the reader will act on the information and wants the stakes first.
  • An update with real consequences, not a subject the reader is meeting for the first time.

Inverted pyramid

Breaking news, wire copy, anything cut from the bottom.

Dateline1 line
Lede1 sentence, 25 to 35 words
Second paragraph1 to 2 sentences
Supporting detail3 to 6 paragraphs
Quote1 to 3 quotes, spread
Context1 to 2 paragraphs
Counterpoint1 paragraph if present
Background tail1 to 3 paragraphs

Pros

  • Cuts cleanly from the bottom at any point and still stands as a complete story.
  • Most newsworthy fact first, so it survives a preview pane or a reader who stops after two paragraphs.
  • One continuous narrative with no labels to maintain.

Cons

  • No flagged significance: the reader has to infer why it matters.
  • A satisfying kicker breaks it; any closing flourish means it no longer cuts from the bottom.
  • Punishes sequence-led stories, where a reconstruction reads flat and out of order.

Use cases

  • Breaking news, court rulings, election results, incident reports.
  • A press release being rewritten as copy that an editor may truncate.
  • Wire-style copy for narrow columns and feed previews.

Anecdotal lede

Features and trend pieces with a human at the centre.

Anecdotal lede2 to 3 paragraphs
Turn1 sentence
Nut graf1 to 2 paragraphs
Evidence2 to 4 paragraphs
Second case1 to 2 paragraphs, usual
Counter-case1 to 2 paragraphs
Causes2 to 3 paragraphs
Return1 to 2 paragraphs

Pros

  • Gives the reader a person to care about before the argument arrives.
  • Alternates scene and thesis, keeping an abstract trend concrete throughout.
  • The return to the opening person closes the loop and lands the change.

Cons

  • The nut graf can fail to land: stay with the person too long and the reader never learns the argument.
  • A decorative scene that does not embody the thesis is just throat-clearing.
  • Needs a real, reported, named case; a composite or hypothetical opening is a bad essay, not this format.

Use cases

  • A trend piece where something is changing and you can show it happening to one person.
  • A feature where the reader has no reason to care yet and needs someone to care about first.
  • Reported work that widens from one named subject to a wider pattern.

Martini glass

Post-mortems and reconstructions with a clear timeline.

Cold open1 to 2 paragraphs
Orientation1 to 2 paragraphs
Narrative60 per cent of the piece
The turn1 paragraph
Analysis2 to 4 paragraphs
Implications2 to 3 paragraphs
Accountability1 to 2 paragraphs
Close1 paragraph

Pros

  • Tells the story once, in order, so the reader has drawn half the analysis before it arrives.
  • The dated sequence is itself the evidence; the timeline carries the argument.
  • A clean gear change at the turn keeps narrative and judgment apart.

Cons

  • It never widens: the piece ends when the story ends, a glass with no base.
  • Analysis leaks into the narrative and spoils the turn ('in what would prove a fatal error').
  • A chronology with silent gaps reads as concealment.

Use cases

  • A post-mortem of an outage, an evacuation, or a collapsed negotiation.
  • A procurement or operational failure with a sourced, datable timeline.
  • A 'how it went wrong' reconstruction where the sequence is the point, not a reveal.

Explainer / FAQ

"What is X", "why does X matter", retrieval reading.

Question headline1 line
Standfirst1 to 2 sentences
What is it?2 to 3 sentences
What just happened?2 to 4 sentences
How does it work?1 to 2 paragraphs or a list
Who does it affect?bullets or 1 paragraph
Why is it controversial?1 to 2 paragraphs if disputed
What happens next?2 to 3 sentences if present
Where can I read more?2 to 4 links

Pros

  • A retrieval structure: the reader lands on the sub-head that matches their question and leaves.
  • Serves a mixed audience, so experts skip ahead while newcomers read from the top.
  • Built to be found by search, with every section independently readable.

Cons

  • Sub-heads drift into labels ('Background', 'Analysis') and the format dies without real questions.
  • Invented questions to fill the shape are pure padding.
  • Flattens a genuine development: the question form strips the news out of a story.

Use cases

  • A 'what is X' on a new regime, treaty, or unfamiliar mechanism.
  • A term suddenly in the news that readers arrive already asking about.
  • Evergreen reference a mixed audience reaches by search rather than in a feed.

Delayed reveal

Investigations where the finding needs context to land.

Anchoring lede1 to 2 paragraphs
Stakes1 to 2 paragraphs
The record2 to 3 paragraphs
Provenance1 to 2 paragraphs
The reveal1 short paragraph
Corroboration2 to 4 paragraphs
Response1 to 2 paragraphs
Consequence2 to 3 paragraphs

Pros

  • Establishes the documents, the verification and the right of reply first, so the finding reads as a finding, not an accusation.
  • The delay buys a contestable fact its weight.
  • Provenance up front pre-empts the reader's 'how would anyone know that'.

Cons

  • A weak reveal makes the whole structure read as manipulation.
  • Delaying past the reader's patience loses everyone before the payoff.
  • Holding back a fact the reader already knows is a trick, not a structure, and they resent it.

Use cases

  • Enterprise reporting on leaked documents or a whistleblower account.
  • A procurement or sanctions-evasion investigation with a contestable finding.
  • A serious allegation whose sourcing needs explaining before it will be believed.

Newsletter skeletons

Several items in one send. The value is in the selection and the order, not in any single item.

Top story + digest

A morning brief with one dominant story.

Masthead line1 line
Lead headline6 words or fewer
Lead lede1 sentence
Why it matters1 to 2 sentences
Driving the news2 to 4 bullets
What's next1 sentence if present
Digest items3 to 6, 40 to 60 words each
Sign-off1 line

Pros

  • The hierarchy is the product: item length itself signals what mattered most.
  • Catches the reader up in three minutes, the lead read properly and the rest skimmed.
  • The lead gets the full Smart Brevity treatment while the digest stays tight.

Cons

  • A flat news day forces a manufactured lead that misleads about the shape of the week.
  • Uneven digest items imply a ranking the reader cannot verify.
  • Past six digest items the reader is scanning, not reading.

Use cases

  • A recurring morning brief on a beat where one story usually dominates.
  • A daily or twice-weekly geopolitics or policy note.
  • A send where readers want the headline story in full and the rest in passing.

Curated list

Flat linkblog, "5 things", Sunday reads.

Title and date1 line
Framing line1 sentence
Item headline1 line repeats
Item summary1 to 2 sentences repeats
Item source1 line repeats
Sign-off1 line

Pros

  • Honest about a flat day: no lead, no false hierarchy.
  • The voice comes entirely from selection, which is the whole editorial act.
  • Equal items make it fast to write and fast to scan.

Cons

  • One 'and relatedly' and you have changed format mid-piece into a narrated digest.
  • One item three times the length of the rest wanted to be a lead, and the edition wanted a top story.
  • A summary that never says why an item was chosen is just a feed the reader could already get.

Use cases

  • A weekend reading list or a '5 things you should know'.
  • A week where several things happened and none dominates.
  • A beat where the value you add is filtering, not synthesis.

Thematic clusters

One beat covered deeply, patterns across items.

Title and period1 line
Standfirst1 to 2 sentences
Cluster heading1 line repeats
Cluster intro1 sentence, a claim repeats
Cluster items2 to 4, 40 to 60 words repeats
Next cluster2 to 4 clusters total
Watch list2 to 3 bullets

Pros

  • Grouping surfaces a direction of travel that the same items scattered through a list would hide.
  • The cluster intro's claim turns a list into pattern recognition.
  • Cluster order carries meaning: lead with the theme the standfirst is about.

Cons

  • Cluster intros that only announce reduce the format to a list with headings, its commonest failure.
  • Fewer than about six items and it is two clusters of two, a list in disguise.
  • Items filed by keyword rather than meaning make a cluster that shows nothing in common.

Use cases

  • One beat covered deeply over a week or fortnight with enough volume to group.
  • A period where you want the reader to notice patterns rather than events.
  • A round-up where several items point the same way and the grouping is the insight.

Narrated digest

Several stories and one argument tying them together.

Title1 line, the argument
Opening claim1 paragraph
First story2 to 3 paragraphs
Transition1 sentence
Second story2 to 3 paragraphs
Transition1 sentence
Further stories1 to 3, 2 paragraphs each
Counter-reading1 paragraph
Synthesis1 to 2 paragraphs
Sourceslist

Pros

  • The only newsletter shape that builds an argument across items instead of aggregating them.
  • Real transitions state a relationship, so each story reads as evidence.
  • Voice is the product; readers come for your reading of events rather than for coverage.

Cons

  • The hardest to fake honestly: force a thesis onto unrelated stories and it is the most annoying newsletter there is.
  • Fake transitions ('meanwhile', 'in other news') expose a list pretending to be an argument.
  • With no counter-reading it reads as a column, not analysis.

Use cases

  • A weekly where you have a thesis and the week's stories are the evidence for it.
  • A commentary newsletter whose readers want interpretation over coverage.
  • A round-up you could defend as an argument if a reader challenged it.
