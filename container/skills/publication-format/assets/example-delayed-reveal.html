The fertiliser runs

Limassol · 9 April · 14 min read

Baltic Meridian Shipping operates eleven vessels from a single floor of an office building near the old port in Limassol [1]. Until 2022 it moved fertiliser, mostly urea, mostly on annual contracts [2], and almost nobody in the industry had heard of it.

It now holds charter contracts the company values at EUR 210m a year [2]. Three of its vessels call at EU ports monthly [3]. Two European insurers carry its hull and machinery cover, and a state-backed export credit agency has guaranteed one of its charters [4].

In its 2024 filings and in a written statement to this publication in January, the company said it had “no counterparties in listed jurisdictions” [2][5]. It said every end user was screened against the consolidated list before a voyage was accepted, and that it had “declined business on this basis on multiple occasions” [5].

Its compliance policy, filed with a Greek charterer in March 2023, commits the company to verifying the discharge terminal, not merely the named consignee [6].

Documents reviewed for this article comprise 2,300 pages of charter records, bunkering invoices and discharge notes covering January 2022 to December 2024 [7]. They were provided by a person with access to the company's operational systems, who is not being identified. Each voyage in the records was checked against commercial port-call data [3] and, for 34 of them, against two insurers' claims correspondence [8].

The records show 41 voyages on which cargo declared as fertiliser was discharged at a terminal operated by a sanctioned entity [7][9].

Port-call data independently places Baltic Meridian vessels alongside that terminal on 38 of the 41 dates [3]. On the remaining three the vessels are recorded at a berth 900 metres away, operated by a subsidiary of the same entity [3][9].

Discharge notes for 22 of the voyages carry a terminal stamp that does not match the consignee named on the corresponding charter [7]. A former operations employee, who left the company in 2024, confirmed the stamp and said the mismatch was “the normal way it was done” [10].

Bunkering invoices place the vessels in the region for a further 14 days in aggregate beyond what the filed voyage plans account for [7].

Asked about the 41 voyages, Baltic Meridian said in a written response that it “relies on documentation provided by charterers” and that any discrepancy in discharge records was “a matter for the terminal operator” [11]. It did not address the mismatch between the discharge stamps and the named consignees. A director declined to answer specific questions on the record. The terminal operator did not respond to three requests for comment.

Both insurers said they were reviewing the cover [12]. Under the January enforcement review, an insurer that continues cover after being put on notice may itself be exposed [13].

The export credit agency confirmed the guarantee is live and said it does not comment on individual exposures [12]. Its governing regulation requires it to suspend a guarantee where it has reasonable grounds to suspect a breach [14], a threshold it has never publicly defined.
