What is the EU's new dual-use export regime, and who does it hit?

Updated 18 March

The EU has extended export licensing to a wider class of civilian electronics and moved the compliance burden onto the intermediaries who move goods rather than the firms that make them [1].

What is a dual-use good?

An item with both civilian and military applications, listed on a control list that the EU updates annually. Most are ordinary: bearings, microcontrollers, navigation modules, certain machine tools. Being dual-use does not make an item banned, only licensable.

What just happened?

On 14 March the Council adopted its nineteenth sanctions package, which listed 42 companies and, separately, widened the control list [1]. Twenty-eight of the 42 are brokers, forwarders or ship managers rather than manufacturers [1]. It is the first package to name firms registered in an EU candidate country [1].

How does the licensing actually work?

In three steps, and the second is where the change bites [2].

  1. The exporter classifies the item against the control list.
  2. If it is listed, the exporter and any intermediary must establish the end user and check them against the consolidated sanctions list. Both parties now carry this duty [1].
  3. The national authority grants, refuses or attaches conditions to a licence.

Who does it affect?

  • Brokers and freight forwarders. Now liable for end-user checks they previously passed through [1]. Roughly 1,400 operate on the EU's eastern frontier, most with fewer than five staff [3].
  • Candidate-country firms. Listed for the first time, which links export enforcement to accession assessments [1].
  • Manufacturers. Less exposed than before, since liability has moved down the chain [2].
  • Insurers. Several have withdrawn cover on affected routes ahead of any listing [4].

Why is it controversial?

Because it puts an enforcement duty on firms that cannot carry it, or because it puts the duty where the knowledge is, depending on who you ask. Supporters, including the Council's sanctions coordinator, argue the broker is the party that actually knows the route [5]. Two member states blocked the package for four weeks, arguing that listing candidate-country entities pre-empts an accession judgment that is not the Council's to make [6].

What happens next?

Member states must report national enforcement measures within 30 days, so by 13 April [1]. The Commission's enlargement report, brought forward to November, will address candidate-country alignment [7]. The next package is expected around June on the established three-month cadence, though no date has been published.
