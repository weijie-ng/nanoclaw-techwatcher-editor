Five things worth reading on the eastern frontier

Everything here is primary reporting or a primary document. Nothing chosen because it was widely shared.

  1. Customs brokers have become the enforcement layer. Small border firms are now making the practical call on whether a controlled shipment moves, with no legal department and no state support. The best account yet of where sanctions policy meets an actual loading bay.
    Baltic Review, Marta Kask, 18 March 2025

  2. The nineteenth package, in full. The Council decision itself, sixty pages. Annex II holds the candidate-country listings and is considerably shorter than the coverage of it suggests. Worth ten minutes before reading anyone's analysis.
    Official Journal of the European Union, L 84/12, 14 March 2025, Annex II

  3. Strait transits fell 14 per cent in February. Monthly port authority data, published without comment. The 14 per cent is the authority's own figure, from table 3, and it is the first hard number under the insurance repricing story everyone has been asserting for a month.
    Regional Port Authority, Monthly Transit Statistics, February 2025, table 3

  4. A registry with no overnight desk. Reconstruction of an October transponder loss in which three monitoring systems all worked and none of them was required to talk to the others. Unusually well documented for this subject.
    Maritime Affairs Quarterly, Sofia Renard, 2 April 2025

  5. The enforcement review that started all of this. January's Commission review, which found controlled goods reaching Russian buyers through third countries at volumes it called materially larger than previously assessed, at page 31. Every package since has followed its logic.
    European Commission, review of third-country circumvention of export controls, January 2025, page 31
