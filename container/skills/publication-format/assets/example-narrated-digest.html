Fortnightly note · 14 March

Enforcement is moving down the supply chain

The interesting thing about this fortnight is not that the EU sanctioned more companies. It is who it sanctioned. Two thirds of the new listings are intermediaries: brokers, forwarders, ship managers [1]. Almost nobody who makes anything.

Start with the package itself. Of the 42 firms named on Tuesday, 28 move goods rather than produce them, and nine were incorporated after February 2022 [1]. The stated rationale is about routes rather than products, and the Council's sanctions coordinator said so out loud at the briefing: the decision to move a shipment is taken by the broker, so that is where the duty should sit [2]. As a theory of enforcement this is coherent. It is also a significant transfer of liability to firms that mostly employ fewer than five people [3].

That would be a policy choice on its own. What makes it a direction of travel is that the commercial system got there first.

Two underwriters withdrew cover for the strait route in February, and a third followed this week [4], all of them before any listing touched the vessels concerned. Correspondent banks cut off two brokerages on the same timeline [5]. None of these are regulatory acts, and none of them can be appealed. If you are a small forwarder, the sanction that ends your business now arrives from your bank, and it arrives first.

Which is why the transit numbers matter more than they look. Transits through the strait fell 14 per cent in February, published by the port authority without comment [6]. That is not a response to a listing, because there was not one yet. It is the market pricing a risk that the regulator had not finished defining.

The complication is that this fortnight the same logic collided with something it was not designed for.

The nineteenth package is the first to name firms in an EU candidate country [1], and the Commission has now moved its enlargement report forward by four months, to November [7]. Two member states are seeking a separate voting route for candidate-country listings [8]. An enforcement instrument aimed at the shipping layer has become an accession question, and nobody involved appears to have planned for that.

The counter-reading is that none of this is strategy, it is capacity. Intermediaries are listed because they are easy to identify: they file, they are registered, they have correspondent banks. End users in third countries are none of those things. On this account the shift down the supply chain says more about what enforcement can see than about what it intends, and it would predict a swing back to manufacturer listings once the intelligence improves. That is the thing to watch in the June package, and it is a genuinely open question.

What it adds up to for now: the compliance burden is being pushed onto firms with five employees and no legal department, by regulators and banks acting in the same direction for different reasons. If the 30-day enforcement reports due on 13 April name intermediaries, that is the model settling in. If they name producers, I have this wrong.
