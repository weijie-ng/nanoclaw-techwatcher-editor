Reconstruction

How three systems watched the same tanker and none of them spoke

Piraeus · 2 April · 11 min read

At 04:12 on 3 October the duty officer at the maritime coordination centre in Piraeus logged a call she had been told would never come. The tanker had switched off its transponder inside the exclusion zone, and it was not answering [1].

The centre had been running the zone's monitoring arrangement since the previous March, under a protocol agreed by seven states and two insurers [2]. On paper the arrangement had three layers. In the four hours that followed, all three of them worked exactly as designed.

The vessel was flagged in April 2023 to a registry that had joined the arrangement two months earlier [3]. Its manager, a company incorporated in Limassol with eleven vessels, filed the required voyage plan for a fertiliser cargo bound for a Black Sea port [4].

In June its original insurer withdrew cover, according to two people with knowledge of the decision [5] and a broker's note reviewed for this article [6]. Cover was replaced within nine days by a provider outside the arrangement, which triggered no notification because none was required [2].

On 1 October the coordination centre's escalation protocol was amended to route transponder losses through the flag state first, a change requested by two participating states to reduce what a meeting record describes as “operational friction” [7].

At 04:12 on 3 October the transponder stopped. The centre notified the flag registry at 04:31. The registry's out-of-hours line recorded the message at 04:33 and forwarded it at 08:10 [1]. The insurer, which held its own tracking feed and had seen the same gap at 04:12, notified nobody, because the protocol did not name it as a notifying party [2][6].

The vessel reappeared at 11:47, seventy-one nautical miles from where it had gone dark, riding higher in the water [8]. Port-call data reviewed for this article places no scheduled discharge in that window [8]. What happened between 04:12 and 11:47 is not established by any record obtained for this article.

Three separate systems saw the tanker that night. None of them was designed to tell the others.

The gap was not technical. Every party held the same position data within minutes of the transponder loss [1][6]. The failure was in who owed a duty to whom: the 1 October amendment made the flag registry the single notifying route [7], and the registry was the one participant with no staffed overnight desk [3].

The amendment's own record shows the risk was raised and set aside. A representative asked what would happen to an out-of-hours alert and was told the registry would “escalate as appropriate”, a phrase that appears nowhere in the protocol's definitions [7][2].

Twelve further vessels operate under the same flag arrangement and the same amended escalation route [3]. Two of the seven participating states have since said they will seek a direct notification channel to insurers [9].

The flag registry declined to answer questions about staffing, saying the matter was under review. The vessel's manager did not respond to four requests for comment. The coordination centre confirmed the timings in this account and declined to discuss the amendment [1].

The protocol is next scheduled for review in September [2].
