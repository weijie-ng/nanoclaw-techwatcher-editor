The customs brokers enforcing Europe's sanctions

Narva, Estonia · 21 March · 8 min read

Marta Ilves runs a customs brokerage out of two rooms above a tyre shop in Narva, forty metres from the Russian border. For nineteen years her work was paperwork: tariff codes, transit forms, the occasional argument about a pallet of machine parts [1].

Last spring she started refusing clients. By September she had turned away eleven of them, four with contracts she had held for a decade. She keeps the refusals in a ring binder, one page each, with the company name and a sentence explaining the decision, which she showed to this reporter [1].

“You look at the end user and you cannot see the end,” she said. “So you say no, and you lose the money, and nobody knows you did it.” [1]

Ilves is not a regulator, and nobody appointed her.

Brokers like her have become the working enforcement layer of European sanctions policy, a role no legislation assigned them and no state funds. As successive packages shift liability from manufacturers to intermediaries [2], the practical decision about whether a controlled shipment moves is increasingly taken by a firm with five employees, no legal department and no way to appeal being wrong [3].

Filings reviewed for this article show roughly 1,400 brokerages operating along the EU's eastern frontier, 62 per cent of them with fewer than five employees [3]. Twenty-eight of the 42 entities named in the bloc's nineteenth sanctions package this month were intermediaries rather than producers [2].

“The legal theory is that the broker knows the trade better than the ministry does,” said Anders Pihl, who advises freight firms on export control in Tallinn. “That happens to be true. It does not follow that the broker can carry the liability.” [4]

Four hundred kilometres south, a forwarder in Latvia who asked not to be named described running three end-user checks on the same consignment of industrial bearings and reaching three different conclusions. He shipped it. “If I am wrong, I find out in two years,” he said [5].

Larger forwarders report the opposite experience. A compliance director at one of the region's three biggest logistics groups said screening had become routine and cheap at scale, and argued that consolidation, not enforcement, was squeezing the small firms [6].

The shift dates to the enforcement review published in January, which found controlled items reaching Russian buyers through third countries in volumes the Commission called materially larger than previously assessed [7]. Every package since has weighted intermediaries more heavily [8].

Ilves has stopped taking new clients in three product categories altogether. The ring binder now runs to nineteen pages [1]. She says no authority has contacted her about any of them; the Estonian tax and customs board declined to say whether it reviews broker refusals at all.
